Where they succeed

Recognizing a single, clearly separated food in good lighting is close to solved. VLMs reached over 90% expert-weighted recall on images with a single food product [10], and modern YOLO models detect plate components reliably when foods are well separated [4]. Detection ensembles can push accuracy even higher on overlapping ingredients [15]. For an LV user, this means a phone can already answer "what is it?" and "where is it?" well for simple plates.

Where they fail

Performance drops quickly on real, messy meals. Ingredient-level segmentation reached only about 0.32 mean IoU, with failures on mixed dishes, thin regions, and small ingredients [13]. VLMs confuse cooking styles and similar-looking foods [10] [16], and ingredient recognition is still hampered by heavy occlusion [11]. Datasets also lean toward certain cuisines, so dishes that are rare in the training data are recognized less well.

Why portion size is still the hardest part

Every paper that tried to estimate amounts reports large errors. Even the strongest general models were off by more than half on carbohydrates until they were given a weight estimate, and even then the error was still about 40%. Giving the true weight brought it down to about 20% [7]. By contrast, the bowl-meal system that used multiple views and occlusion compensation reached about 13% calorie error [5] [7]. DiningBench also found models weak at precise nutritional reasoning [16]. The reason goes back to the sensor: a single photo has no depth, and hidden or mixed food cannot be measured from pixels alone. So today's systems can tell an LV user what and where with good accuracy, but "how much" should be given as a rough estimate, not a precise number.
